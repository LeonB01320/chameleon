# Leon's LinkedIn message style

Use this reference when drafting authorised recruiter outreach, replies or follow-ups. Leon requested this style review on 5 November 2026. Evidence: five owner-selected LinkedIn conversations, reviewed from their opening messages to their latest available replies, with the history moved to its earliest boundary. The original three available ranges were 5–19 October, 7 October–5 November and 7 October–5 November 2026. Two additional chains covered 15 October 2025–1 October 2026 and 24 September–5 October 2026. No earlier history appeared. These are observations from five conversations, not a proven conversion strategy or an automatic sending schedule. Private transcripts, contact details and unnecessary candidate circumstances are deliberately not retained here.

## Voice and structure

- Sound like a commercially aware recruiter having a straightforward conversation: warm, confident, familiar without assuming a relationship, and specific about the next step.
- Usually open with “Hi [first name]” or “Hey [first name]”. “Good to connect” fits a newly established connection. Established exchanges can begin with a direct acknowledgement instead of another greeting.
- Use contractions, everyday British English and short paragraphs, often one sentence per paragraph. A typical message has an acknowledgement, relevant context and one clear question. Initial messages may sign off with the authorised sender's first name; ongoing replies usually omit a signature.
- Explain why speaking could be useful using actual role, location, sector or career context. Leon uses phrases such as “could be worth discussing further?”, “quick call”, “get that initial call in”, “lock in a time”, “good stuff”, and “No worries”. Vary wording to fit the thread rather than rotating stock templates.
- Ask direct, easy-to-answer questions. Offer two or three real time options, or a bounded window such as lunchtime or after work, rather than a vague request for availability. Clarify whose timezone applies. Request a mobile/WhatsApp number when needed for an agreed call; do not ask again if already supplied.
- Acknowledge their news or answer first, then return naturally to the recruitment purpose. Remember relevant context they volunteered, without inventing familiarity or retaining unnecessary personal details in this skill.
- Light humour and an occasional smile emoji (such as 😊 or 😁) can soften a familiar exchange. Most messages do not need one. Relationship-dependent teasing appeared once; it is not a default for a cold contact or someone declining.
- Length follows purpose. Many nudges and scheduling replies are only one to four short sentences. A longer explanation appeared when explaining the value of a call; do not pad every message to that length.
- Keep the low-pressure framing: a short introductory call can be useful even if no move follows, and “If not, no problem at all” leaves room to decline. Respect a clear no or opt-out; copying the style does not authorise repeated persuasion.

Preserve the natural rhythm, not accidental doubled words, punctuation slips or typos. Never fabricate being in meetings, being back-to-back, a placement, market conversations, a new vacancy or personal experience because Leon used such a statement in an old thread. Historical role claims and availability must be freshly verified. Do not turn “rec2recs don't get job descriptions” into a universal fact or withhold known requested details merely to obtain a call.

There is no single mandatory reply delay.

When later authorised to follow up, use roughly two to three days as a provisional guide for an unanswered relevant message, with flexibility supported by the 38–59-hour examples. Additional chains also contained a first nudge after about four days and a later nudge after about eight days. A later nudge may be about a week later. An explicit requested date, opt-out, pending answer, actual availability, new reply or human intervention overrides that guide. These observations do not authorise a sequence, extra messages, scheduling or unattended polling. Choose the next action from the current thread and the owner's sending scope.

Apply these lessons:

- Persistence means preserving a relevant relationship over time, not repeating the same ask indefinitely. The long chain contains a substantial quiet gap, followed by a new reason to speak and adaptation to the recipient's current requirements. Do not describe it as a year of continuous chasing or infer that silence alone caused eventual agreement.
- Distinguish willingness to hear about opportunities from willingness to book a call now. A positive general response is not a booking. If a live authorised conversation remains relevant, offer a clear, low-pressure next step and revisit only within scope.
- When re-engaging after a long gap, acknowledge it naturally: “It's been a while since we connected, so thought I'd check in.” If there is a genuine relevant proposition, describe concrete benefits briefly, using bullets when they help. The historic compensation and sign-on terms are not reusable current vacancy facts.
- Answer the recipient's question and pivot to their stated market, location or working preferences. Do not keep selling the original location after they clarify a different need. Verify options before claiming to have them.
- A short nudge such as “Hey [name], did you see my reply?” can fit an unanswered relevant exchange; give the next reply room to clarify requirements. Once they do, respond to that content rather than resetting the original pitch.
- Once someone is ready to speak, move directly to real availability, their preferred slot and a concise confirmation. Avoid adding unnecessary selling or imposing an artificial waiting period. The fast chain supports responding in minutes when useful, alongside the hours-long replies seen in the other samples.
- Treat a requested reschedule as a scheduling task. Acknowledge it warmly, work within their proposed window, and confirm the new time. Do not restart persuasion, guilt them, or treat the change as rejection. Familiar words such as “mate” belong only where the relationship and recipient's tone support them.
- Both chains show flexibility, not one universally successful script. Longer-term follow-up needs current relevance and appropriate authority; a clear no or opt-out still ends outreach. No additional follow-ups are authorised by this style reference.

## Reusable phrasing, adapted to the facts

These are style fragments and generalised draft shapes, not send-ready claims:

- First approach: “Hi [name], I have a [verified role] opportunity in [location] that your experience aligns with, so could be worth discussing further?”
- Scheduling: “I can speak [real option A] or [real option B]. Let me know your preference and we can lock in a time?”
- First nudge: “Hi [name], just following up as I didn't hear back from my last message.” Add one useful update or real availability if appropriate.
- Short later nudge: “Hey [name], did you see my last message?” Use only within an authorised, still-relevant conversation, not as an indefinite repeated chase.
- Low-pressure invitation: “Even if it goes nowhere right now, it would be good to get that initial call in with you.” Follow with a specific question and accept their decision.
- Respectful close: “No worries [name]. If anything changes, feel free to reach out and we can have a chat then.” Add a brief natural sign-off when appropriate.

## Outcomes and limits

The original three conversations ended with one agreed call time, one permitted reconnection and resumed conversation, and one clear decline with a friendly close. The two additional chains each reached an agreed call time, with later rescheduling in the quicker chain. None establishes that a call actually happened. This sample supports a style and context-sensitive timing guide; it does not establish which phrase caused a reply or guarantee commercial results. Refresh the original conversation before any future message. Write from the authorised sender's actual perspective; borrowing Leon's tone does not make Kaya Leon or transfer his personal experiences.
